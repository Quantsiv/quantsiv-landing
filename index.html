01

The problem

Harvest now, decrypt later, digital forge later and AI assistant prompt mistakes.

Two quantum threats

Harvest now, decrypt later

Today, hackers can intercept and store encrypted data, then decrypt it with sufficiently powerful quantum computers in the future. This puts today's encryption and key-exchange systems, such as RSA and Diffie-Hellman, at risk. It all depends on how long the data must stay secret. If it has to stay private for 25 years, traffic recorded yesterday is already at risk.

Digital signature forgery

Digital signatures make it possible to check that software, certificates, documents and transactions are genuine. A quantum computer could derive private keys from the public ones and forge signatures. Signatures must stay trustworthy, including those in devices, certificate authorities and permanent records. For crypto-asset wallets, a public key exposed on-chain could also make unauthorized transfers possible.

Error-prone AI agents

When AI agents generate code, they tend to fall back on patterns and defaults they already know. Those patterns often rely on vulnerable algorithms. AI assistant code can work correctly and pass its tests while still being exposed. In a controlled study, developers using an AI assistant wrote less secure code (Perry, Srivastava, Kumar and Boneh, ACM CCS 2023).

02

Deadlines

US and EU executive orders and recommendation.

The deadlines are already set

These are not just predictions. In the US and the EU, institutions have published guidance and requirements with set dates for replacing vulnerable cryptography.

  1. 13 August 2024

    NIST publishes the new standards

    The US standards institute publishes three new standards: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), to establish the keys used to encrypt data, and FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA), for digital signatures. Alternatives to today's algorithms are therefore available.

  2. November 2024

    NIST proposes deadlines for retiring today's algorithms

    In the initial public draft of NIST IR 8547, all quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms, including P-256, are listed as disallowed after 2035.

  3. 22 June 2026

    Executive Order 14412

    The order addresses the risk known as “harvest now, decrypt later”. For high-value assets and high-impact systems, it sets 31 December 2030 as the deadline for post-quantum key establishment and 31 December 2031 for digital signatures.

  4. 24 June 2026

    OMB M-26-15: a phased federal migration

    Federal agencies must build an automated inventory whose data “should populate a central Cryptographic Bill of Materials” (CBOM). Agency plans are due by 22 October 2026. The migration runs in five phases, from 2026 to 2035.

  5. About December 2026

    A proposed rule for federal contractors

    EO 14412 gives the FAR Council 180 days, until about 19 December 2026, to propose a rule under the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR). The rule would require covered contractors to comply by 31 December 2030 with NIST's FIPS standards, including those based on post-quantum algorithms.

  6. About March 2027

    Guidance on what a CBOM should contain

    Within 270 days of EO 14412, around March 2027, CISA, in coordination with NIST, is due to publish guidance on the minimum elements a CBOM should include.

  7. 2030 to 2035

    The migration deadlines

    Under EO 14412, post-quantum key establishment must be adopted by 31 December 2030, and digital signatures by 31 December 2031, on high-value assets and high-impact systems. If the FAR rule the order calls for is adopted, covered contractors must comply by 31 December 2030. NSA CNSA 2.0 sets exclusive-use dates between 2030 and 2033, depending on the category. In the EU, the NIS Cooperation Group roadmap of 23 June 2025, a recommendation, asks member states to set national strategies by the end of 2026, address high-risk use cases by the end of 2030 and migrate as many systems as feasible by 2035.

Sources: EO 14412; EO 14412 in the Federal Register; OMB M-26-15; NIST IR 8547; CISA Post-Quantum Cryptography Initiative; NSA CNSA 2.0; NIST FIPS 203; EU NIS Cooperation Group roadmap.

How it works

An easy four-step process.

How Quantsiv Works

Four steps, from scanning your code to a standard record (a CycloneDX 1.6 CBOM).

1. Discover

Quantsiv runs in your CI by default, so your code stays in your environment, and Quantsiv receives the CBOM and the finding details only if you choose to upload them. Quantsiv also scans public repositories on its own servers.*

2. Analyze

Each finding is checked against quantum-vulnerable algorithms and ranked by its retention period.

3. Rank

For each finding, Quantsiv explains the priority and the standard to migrate to.

4. Document

Quantsiv generates a CycloneDX 1.6 CBOM, a SARIF file that works with code-analysis tools, and a report with the findings ranked by priority. An AI agent uses this information to suggest quantum-safe alternatives and help plan the changes.

*Private repositories are not scanned externally.

* Real Quantsiv results from a demo repository

Features

Built for the quantum era.

Support for many languages

Detects the most common cryptographic APIs in Python, JavaScript, TypeScript, Java, Kotlin, Go, C, C++, C# and Swift, plus openssl, ssh-keygen and keytool commands in build scripts.*

  • Python
  • JavaScript
  • TypeScript
  • Java
  • Kotlin
  • Go
  • C
  • C++
  • C#

Cryptographic inventory (CBOM)

Automatically creates a standard CycloneDX 1.6 CBOM or imports CBOMs generated by other tools.

Risk assessment

Ranks encryption by how long the data must stay secret, and digital signatures by how long they must be trusted. The most urgent issues are highlighted first.

Checks on every build

Automatically flags, on every build, the vulnerable cryptography a change introduces. Brings the checks into pull requests for early feedback.

Simple CI/CD integration

Works with GitHub Actions, GitLab, Jenkins and Azure DevOps. Run scans on your own computer or inside the pipeline you already use.

  • GitHub Actions
  • GitLab
  • Jenkins
  • Azure DevOps

Keys, certificates and TLS

Recognizes the algorithms and key sizes in key and certificate files, and probes the TLS connections of the servers you name with one handshake.

Infrastructure configuration

Finds vulnerable key and TLS settings in Terraform, Kubernetes, CloudFormation, web-server, OpenSSL and OpenSSH configuration in your repository.

Crypto wallet support

Finds secp256k1 and Ed25519 signing keys, Schnorr signatures, and ECDH and ECIES key exchange in common wallet libraries.

MCP support

Through a read-only MCP connector, lets AI coding assistants check your cryptography rules while they write code.

Post-quantum migration suggestions

Suggests the best-fitting standard: ML-KEM, ML-DSA or SLH-DSA, or a hybrid key exchange.

AI agent analysis

Siv. An AI agent able to analyze each finding in its context, proposing a fix. It connects to the model of your choosing through its chat-completions endpoint, for example ChatGPT, Claude, DeepSeek or Grok, or one you host yourself. No change is merged without the user's approval.

*Coverage varies by language. Pattern matching misses aliased and dynamic calls and third-party code, and does not read cloud key-management services.
