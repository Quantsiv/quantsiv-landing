Two Quantum Threats

A large enough quantum computer could read data copied today and forge the signatures people and machines rely on. Two quantum threats, two clocks. Quantsiv ranks your cryptography against both, on every build.

Harvest Now, Decrypt Later

Attackers can record encrypted traffic and copy encrypted data today, then decrypt it once a large enough quantum computer exists. This threatens encryption and key exchange, such as RSA encryption and Diffie-Hellman. The deadline is how long the data must stay secret: if it must stay confidential for 25 years, traffic recorded yesterday is already at risk.

Signature Forgery

Trust Now, Forge Later

Signatures prove that a software update, a certificate, a document or a transaction is genuine. Nothing has to be stolen first, because the public key is public by design. A large enough quantum computer could work out the private key from it and sign in your name, so a fake looks genuine. This threatens signatures such as RSA, ECDSA and EdDSA. The deadline is how long a signature or key must stay trusted: keys built into devices, certificate authorities, and records that must remain proof. For crypto-asset wallets, a public key already on-chain is enough to forge a transfer.

AI coding assistants can repeat vulnerable defaults

Code assistants choose cryptography by completing familiar patterns, so they can repeat old defaults that a quantum computer could break. The code works and passes its tests, and it is still vulnerable.

The Deadline Is Already Set

These are published dates, not predictions. Standards bodies and governments in the US and the EU have issued dated guidance and requirements for replacing the vulnerable cryptography.

  1. 13 Aug 2024

    NIST publishes the replacement standards

    The US standards institute publishes three new algorithms: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) to agree the keys that encrypt data, and FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) for digital signatures. The replacements now exist.

  2. Nov 2024

    NIST proposes when today's algorithms retire

    In the initial public draft of NIST IR 8547, all quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms, including P-256, are disallowed after 2035.

  3. 22 Jun 2026

    Executive Order 14412

    The order addresses the harvest-now, decrypt-later threat. It sets 31 Dec 2030 for post-quantum key establishment and 31 Dec 2031 for signatures on high-value assets and high-impact systems.

  4. 24 Jun 2026

    OMB M-26-15: the federal migration in phases

    Federal agencies build an automated inventory whose data "should populate a central Cryptographic Bill of Materials". Agency plans are due 22 Oct 2026. Five phases from 2026 to 2035.

  5. ~Dec 2026

    A proposed rule for federal contractors

    EO 14412 gives the FAR Council 180 days, about 19 Dec 2026, to propose a Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) rule requiring covered contractors to comply by 31 Dec 2030 with NIST's FIPS standards, including those that use post-quantum algorithms.

  6. ~Mar 2027

    Guidance on what a CBOM should contain

    CISA, the US cybersecurity agency, in coordination with NIST, publishes guidance on CBOM minimum elements within 270 days of EO 14412, about Mar 2027.

  7. 2030 – 2035

    Migration deadlines run to 2035

    Under EO 14412, key establishment by 31 Dec 2030 and signatures by 31 Dec 2031 on high-value assets and high-impact systems, and, once the FAR rule the order calls for is adopted, 31 Dec 2030 for covered contractors. NSA CNSA 2.0 sets exclusive-use dates between 2030 and 2033, depending on the category. In the EU, the NIS Cooperation Group roadmap (23 Jun 2025, a recommendation) asks for national strategies by end-2026, high-risk use cases by end-2030 and as many systems as feasible by 2035.

Sources: EO 14412, EO 14412 in the Federal Register, OMB M-26-15, NIST IR 8547, CISA Post-Quantum Cryptography Initiative, NSA CNSA 2.0, NIST FIPS 203, EU NIS Cooperation Group roadmap.

How Quantsiv Works

Four steps, from scanning your code to a standard record of its cryptography (a CycloneDX 1.6 CBOM).

1. Discover

Quantsiv looks for cryptography in source code, key and certificate files, configuration and infrastructure files, build scripts, and the secure connections (TLS) of servers you name or prove you own. Only the CBOM and the details of each finding reach Quantsiv, and only if you upload them.

2. Analyze

Each finding is checked against the algorithms a quantum computer could break: RSA, ECDSA, Diffie-Hellman and DSA. Encryption and key exchange are ranked by how long you say the data must stay secret, the harvest-now-decrypt-later risk. Signatures are ranked on their own track, by how long you say they must stay trusted, against the signature deadline.

3. Prioritize

A health record that must stay secret for years and a short-lived login token are not the same risk, even when both use RSA. Each finding says why it ranks where it does and, where the use is clear, which standard to move to.

4. Prove

Quantsiv writes a CycloneDX 1.6 CBOM, a SARIF file for code-scanning tools and a ranked report. Each build is compared with the latest build of your main branch, and the change check flags cryptography a change adds, so the migration does not slide back.
